THE HOUSE THAT COUNTED
A short tale of things that wait behind walls
A work of quiet horror


THE HOUSE THAT COUNTED

The house at the end of Bellweather Lane had been empty for
twenty-seven years. Everyone in town knew this because everyone had
helped empty it. They had carried out the furniture, boxed the books,
stripped the curtains from the windows and watched the removal men
take the grandfather clock down the front steps.

Yet every night at 2:13, a light appeared in the upstairs landing
window. It stayed on for exactly one minute. No one had ever seen
anyone enter. No one had ever seen anyone leave.

Mara Vale had lived opposite the house since she was six. She was
thirty now, and the light had never missed a night. Her mother used to
say it was a wiring fault. Her father said old houses remembered their
habits. After he died, Mara stopped asking either of them.

On the first Monday of November, a letter arrived with no stamp and
no return address. Inside was a brass key and a strip of paper. The
handwriting was her father's, though he had been dead for eleven years.

DON'T LET IT FINISH COUNTING, it said.


1. THE KEY

Mara crossed the road at dusk. The house looked smaller up close, but
the darkness behind its windows seemed deeper than the street could
explain. Ivy covered the porch pillars. A narrow crack split the front
door from top to bottom, as if something inside had been pressing its
ear against it.

The brass key turned without resistance. The door opened inward. The
air smelled of wet stone and the faint sweetness of flowers left too long
in a vase.

She called out. Her voice travelled down the hall and came back
thinner, carrying a second sound beneath it: a soft click, like a tongue
touching the roof of a mouth.

The hallway was bare except for a row of pencil marks on the
wallpaper. They began at knee height and climbed toward the ceiling.
Each mark had a date beside it. The earliest was dated 1986. The latest
was tomorrow.

Mara touched the newest mark. The pencil line was warm.


2. THE LANDING

There were thirteen steps to the first floor. Mara counted them twice.
On the landing stood a narrow table and, above it, a mirror with a black
cloth draped over its frame.

The light switch did nothing. Still, at the far end of the landing, a
yellow glow leaked from beneath a closed door.

She knew the room. It had been her father's study, though he had never
owned this house. As a child, she had once dreamed of standing in that
room while a man behind the door counted backward from one
hundred. She had woken at zero with her father's hand over her mouth.

The door opened a finger's width. Inside, a desk lamp illuminated a
ledger. Its pages were filled with names, each followed by a number.
Some names had one. Some had hundreds. Her own name appeared
near the bottom.

MARA VALE — 29.

Beside it, in fresh ink, someone had written: 30.


3. THE VOICE IN THE WALL

A whisper came from behind the wallpaper. It sounded like a woman
speaking through a mouthful of water.

"You are early."

Mara backed away. The whisper moved with her, travelling inside the
wall. A second voice joined it. Then a third. They spoke her name in
different tones: her mother's tired voice, her father's low murmur, her
own voice as a little girl.

She found a loose strip of wallpaper and pulled. Beneath it was a small
square of wood, polished by years of touch. A keyhole had been carved
into its centre. The brass key fit.

Inside the wall was a staircase descending into darkness. On the first
step lay a child's red mitten. Mara recognised it. She had lost it during
the winter her younger brother disappeared.

She had never told anyone that, in the weeks after he vanished, she
could hear him counting from inside her bedroom wall.


4. THE ROOM BELOW

The staircase ended in a room that was too large to fit beneath the
house. Its ceiling disappeared into blackness. Hundreds of clocks hung
from wires. None had hands. Each had a small glass window where the
numbers should have been.

Behind a desk sat a woman in a grey dress. Her face was hidden by a
veil of fine white thread. She turned a page in a book although the book
was empty.

"Your father borrowed time," the woman said. "He thought grief was a
door. He thought he could open it and bring someone back."

Mara's throat tightened. "My brother?"

"The house does not return what it takes. It returns what it has learned
to imitate."

The clocks began to tick. One by one, their glass windows filled with
numbers. Every clock showed 29.

The woman lifted her veiled head. "It has nearly finished counting
you."


5. THE RULE

"What happens at thirty?" Mara asked.

The woman placed a finger on the empty book. "A place is made. A
name is removed. The house becomes quiet again."

"How do I stop it?"

"You must give it the number it has been waiting for."

Mara looked at the clocks. "Thirty?"

"No. The first number."

The woman opened the book. A single word appeared on the page,
written in the handwriting from the letter: ELIAS.

Her father's name.

Mara understood then. He had not been trying to bring her brother
back. He had been trying to trade places with him. The house had
accepted the bargain, but it had counted the wrong person. It had been
counting the family ever since.

From somewhere above them came three knocks. Then a child's voice:

"Mara? You took your time."


6. THE FIRST NUMBER

The voice belonged to her brother. It was exactly as she remembered it:
bright, impatient, almost laughing.

Mara stepped toward the staircase. The veiled woman caught her wrist.
Her fingers were cold and surprisingly strong.

"It knows the shape of what you miss," she warned. "That is not the
same as knowing them."

Mara turned back to the clocks. On the nearest one, the number 1
appeared. Beneath it, a tiny brass plate bore her father's name.

She took the clock from its hook and smashed it against the desk.

Every clock stopped. The woman made a sound like a hundred pages
tearing. The room folded inward. The staircase stretched, then snapped
back into place.

Above, the house gave a long, wooden groan. The light on the landing
went out.

For the first time in twenty-seven years, Bellweather Lane was
completely dark.


CODA. THE EMPTY HOUSE

The police found Mara on the pavement at dawn. She was barefoot,
soaked through and holding a brass key so tightly that its teeth had cut
her palm.

The house was empty. There was no staircase behind the wallpaper, no
study, no ledger. The upstairs landing was bare except for a mirror
covered in black cloth.

Mara moved away three weeks later. She did not tell anyone where she
was going. She did not keep the key.

Years passed. The house was demolished. A supermarket replaced it.
The people who worked there complained about a light that flickered in
the stockroom at 2:13 every morning. Management blamed faulty
wiring.

On the twenty-ninth anniversary of the night Mara entered the house, a
little boy found a brass key beneath a shelf of canned peaches. A strip
of paper was tied to it with thread.

The boy could not read the handwriting, so he took it to his mother.
She went pale when she saw the words.

DON'T LET IT FINISH COUNTING.

That night, at 2:13, every clock in the supermarket stopped. In the dark
stockroom, something began counting aloud.

It started at one.
